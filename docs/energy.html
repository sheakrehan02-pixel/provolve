Where does the energy come from?

Put an atom on a scale and it weighs less than the parts it's made of. You start knowing nothing. You finish having calculated, by hand, the energy that powers the Sun.

Here's the claim this issue will prove: nuclear energy isn't burning anything. When the particles in an atom's core rearrange into a tighter shape, a tiny amount of their mass disappears, and that mass comes out as energy. Coal, gasoline and wood all work by rearranging electrons. Nuclear reactions rearrange the core itself, and that's why they're millions of times stronger.

Level 1Play · the missing mass✓ cleared

Weigh an atom against its own parts.

Every atom has a tiny core, the nucleus, built from two kinds of particles: protons and neutrons. A proton weighs 1.007276 atomic mass units (u). A neutron weighs 1.008665 u. So a nucleus should just weigh the sum of its parts.

It doesn't. Put the loose parts on one side of a scale and the finished nucleus on the other, and the nucleus is lighter. Every time.

The missing mass has a name: the mass defect. It didn't vanish. When the parts snapped together, they let go of energy, and energy carries mass with it. Run it backwards and you'd have to put that energy back in to pull the nucleus apart. That energy is called the binding energy.

Level 2Play · glue versus push✓ cleared

Why some atoms hold on tighter than others.

Inside a nucleus there's a tug of war. A very strong glue, the strong nuclear force, holds each particle to the ones right next to it, and only those. Meanwhile every proton is positively charged, so every proton pushes every other proton away, all the way across the nucleus.

Small nuclei are mostly surface, so many particles have few neighbours to hold. Big nuclei have lots of protons all pushing on each other. Somewhere in the middle, the glue wins by the most. Build nuclei and find where.

Level 4Play · the valley of iron✓ cleared

Everything wants to roll down to iron.

Take the glue-per-particle number from Level 2 and plot it for every atom, upside down. You get a valley. The lower an atom sits, the more tightly it's held, and iron is at the bottom.

Any reaction that moves atoms downhill releases energy. Heavy atoms roll down by splitting, which is called fission. Light atoms roll down by joining, which is called fusion. Try both.

Notice the asymmetry. Splitting one uranium atom releases about 170 to 200 MeV. Fusing hydrogen releases only 17.6 MeV, but it uses far fewer particles, so per kilogram fusion wins by about four times. And iron gives you nothing either way. That's why stars that make iron in their cores are about to die.

Level 5Derive · four steps✓ cleared

The proof, one step at a time.

You've seen that mass goes missing, and that the tighter a nucleus is held, the more mass it lost. Now the exact link between missing mass and energy.

STEP 1 · MASS IS ENERGY

Einstein's result from 1905: mass and energy are the same thing measured in different units. The conversion factor is the speed of light, squared.

DebriefYour intuition vs. the physics

How did your gut do?

If fusion is so good, why don't we have it yet?

A · HeatNuclei repel each other. To get close enough to fuse, the fuel has to be around 100 million °C, hotter than the Sun's core.
B · Holding itNothing solid can touch fuel that hot. Reactors hold it in magnetic fields, and keeping it stable is very hard.
C · FuelTritium barely exists in nature. Reactors will have to breed their own from lithium.
D · MaterialsThe 14 MeV neutrons from each reaction slowly damage the walls of the machine.

That's the whole method of nuclear physics in miniature: weigh the before and after, find the mass that went missing, and multiply by c².
