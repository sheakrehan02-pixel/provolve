    The patient you cannot see

    A beginner's journey from one differential equation to the mathematics of hidden biological systems, uncertainty, and personalized medicine.

    Inside the body · hidden
    hidden biologythe modelmeasurementswhat you controluncertainty
    ~45 min15 levelsAlgebra → personalized models

    I give you five numbers: a heart rate, a blood measurement, a biomarker concentration, an imaging reading, and the same blood measurement six hours later. Then I ask: what is happening inside this person's body?

    You can't simply look inside and read off the number of diseased cells, the strength of an immune response, how much drug reached a tissue, or the hidden settings of a feedback loop. Medicine gives you a handful of measurements. The biology that produced them stays hidden. Mathematics asks: what hidden system could have produced these measurements?

    You cannot measure everything.

    Wait — why can two models fit the same data?
    Five points in a short window only pin down how the system behaves in that window. Any model flexible enough to bend through them fits. The models disagree about what happens after, which is exactly what you didn't measure. That's why "it fits" is the start of a scientific argument, not the end.

    Wait — why can't we just collect more data?
    More of the same kind of measurement only pins down more precisely. The valley gets narrower but stays a line. You need data that depends on and differently. In medicine that might mean a second biomarker, a different time point, or a deliberate perturbation, like a test dose.

    Each test narrows the belief. Turn the noise up and it narrows more slowly, and sometimes a noisy test even shifts it the wrong way for a while. The output isn't a single magic number; it's a distribution of possibilities, and the haze around the concentration curve is that uncertainty, carried forward into a prediction.

    Mathematical medicine doesn't always eliminate uncertainty. Sometimes its job is to represent uncertainty honestly.
    Wait — why do we need probability?
    Because with noise, many values of k are consistent with what you saw, just not equally. A single "best fit" throws away how much the data actually constrain the answer. Probability keeps that information, and you need it to make a decision like a dose, where being wrong in one direction is worse than in the other.

    You aren't merely changing a number. You're changing the structure of the system's possible futures.
    Wait — why can a tiny parameter change create a huge outcome?
    At a fold, a stable state and an unstable one collide and annihilate (exactly here). Just before, the valley holding the system is shallow; just after, it doesn't exist. The change in is tiny; the change in the landscape is total.

    11Ill-posed problems · regularization✓ cleared

    Mathematics refusing to overreact

    Try to estimate 50 parameters for one person from 10 measurements and you've made a monster: more unknowns than information. That's an ill-posed inverse problem. A solution may not exist, may not be unique, or may swing wildly when the data change slightly. More mathematical sophistication doesn't automatically create more information. One defense:

    The best model isn't the one that hugs every data point. It balances evidence against complexity: Occam's razor, written as math. And a model can be wrong in two different ways. Wrong parameters: better data fixes it. Wrong structure: the equations themselves are wrong, and no amount of fitting can save it. You can have a tiny fitting error and still a biologically misleading model. That's why validation matters.

    Wait — why doesn't a perfect fit mean a correct model?
    With ten parameters and ten points, a polynomial can pass through every point exactly, including through the noise. The training error hits zero precisely because the model memorized the measurement errors. On a new patient, those memorized errors are wrong, and the predictions swing wildly between the points.

    Wait — why can't AI simply learn everything?
    A learned model is only as good as the situations in its training data. Change the drug, the population, or the hospital's measuring equipment, and it can fail silently, still printing a confident number. A mechanistic model can reason about new situations, but only if its mechanism is right. Neither escapes the need to check predictions against reality.
    13Multiscale · the impossible diagram✓ cleared

    Descend through a human being

    A body can be modeled at many scales: molecules, cells, tissues, organs, physiology, the whole patient. The scales talk to each other in both directions. That's a multiscale system, and connecting models across its levels, with honest uncertainty at each one, is one of the frontiers of systems biology. Zoom down through the levels and open any node.

    Instrument 13 · The impossible diagramWhat's measurable, and what's hidden, at each scale?

    This is where the buzzword arrives: a digital twin, a computational model linked to one real person and updated as new data come in. Patient-specific 3D anatomy models already help surgeons plan operations; research is exploring "virtual patients" for testing treatment strategies. But a digital twin is not a perfect copy of a human. It's a model, and models have boundaries. The real question is how much useful information about one person can be encoded in a model, and how reliably that model can support a decision.

    The final paradox

    Can mathematics tell us what's happening inside a patient? Sometimes. But the more interesting discovery: mathematics doesn't make the invisible visible by magic. It gives you a structured way of reasoning about it. You observe, model, infer, simulate, test, collect better data, revise, and repeat.

    Differential equations describe change. Dynamical systems describe behavior. Optimization estimates parameters. Probability describes uncertainty. Bayesian inference updates beliefs. Identifiability says what the data can actually reveal. Control theory asks how interventions alter trajectories. Multiscale modeling connects levels of biology. Machine learning finds patterns. Mechanistic modeling encodes hypotheses about causes. Personalized modeling asks whether all of it can be brought together for one person.

    That direction is easy. The reverse is the whole field:

    A hundred years from now a doctor might ask a patient's model "what happens if we perturb this system?" and watch thousands of simulated futures. The fundamental question will remain: how do we know the model is describing the person, rather than our assumptions about the person? That's a medical question, a mathematical one, a philosophical one, and finally a question about the limits of knowledge.

    How did your gut do?

    Medicine is fundamentally an inference problem: the variables we care about are often hidden, the measurements we get are incomplete, and mathematics is the machinery we use to reason backward from observable consequences to invisible mechanisms.
