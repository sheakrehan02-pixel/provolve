Can you build a wormhole?

You start knowing nothing. You finish having derived, by hand, the equation that tells physicists why a traversable wormhole needs matter that nobody has ever bottled.

One honest thing before we start: physicists have not proved that wormholes are impossible. Einstein's equations allow certain wormhole shapes on paper. What the math shows is that keeping one open, so a person could actually walk through, would demand a kind of energy that ordinary matter doesn't have. Whether nature has any of it is still an open question. By the end, you'll see exactly where that demand comes from.

Level 1Play · escape speed✓ cleared

Throw a ball hard enough and it never comes back.

Throw a ball up and gravity slows it, stops it, and pulls it back down. Throw it faster and it goes higher. On Earth, at about 11.2 km/s, it never returns. That speed is called escape velocity.

Escape velocity depends on how much mass there is and how close you are to its center. Keep the mass the same, squeeze it smaller, and the escape speed climbs. Squeeze far enough and escape needs more than the speed of light. Since nothing goes faster than light, nothing gets out. That's a black hole.

Level 2Play · gravity is geometry✓ cleared

Gravity isn't a pull. It's a shape.

Newton described gravity as a force between objects. Einstein replaced that with something stranger: space and time form a single four-dimensional fabric, spacetime, and mass and energy bend it. Objects then follow the straightest possible paths through that bent fabric, and those paths look like orbits and falls.

The physicist John Wheeler compressed it into two halves: matter tells spacetime how to curve, and spacetime tells matter how to move.

The no-go resultA static, traversable wormhole in classical general relativity requires matter that violates the null energy condition at its throat. Ordinary matter can't hold it open.

Read that carefully. We didn't assume exotic matter; the geometry forced it on us. And we proved a conditional result. Quantum fields can produce small, brief pockets of negative energy (the Casimir effect is a measured example), and modified theories of gravity change the rules. None of that has yet produced a usable, human-sized wormhole, but it keeps the question open.

Even with exotic matter, four walls remain

A · EnergyWe don't know how to make NEC-violating matter in the amount and shape a wormhole needs.
B · Quantum limitsQuantum inequalities cap how much negative energy can pile up, and for how long.
C · StabilityA solution that exists might collapse at the slightest nudge. That takes a separate perturbation analysis.
D · CausalitySome wormhole setups allow loops in time. Topological censorship theorems limit what's possible under stated assumptions.

That's the whole method of theoretical physics in miniature: propose a shape, run Einstein's equation backwards, then check whether the required stuff could exist.
