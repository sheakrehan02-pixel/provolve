Chicago, Ill.Wednesday, December 2, 19429:45 a.m.

The day Fermi switched on the first reactor

It's below freezing outside. Under the west stands of the University of Chicago's football stadium, 49 people are crowded into an old squash court around a pile of black bricks. You're one of them.

The thing in front of you is called Chicago Pile-1, and "pile" is exactly right. It's a squashed ball of graphite bricks about 7.5 metres wide, with lumps of uranium tucked inside. Wooden rods coated in cadmium slide through it. Cadmium soaks up neutrons like a sponge, so with the rods in, the pile sleeps.

Up on a platform, three young physicists hold buckets of cadmium solution, ready to drown the pile if something goes wrong. One man stands by with an axe, next to a rope that holds up an emergency rod. Everyone else is watching the neutron counters. The man in charge, Enrico Fermi, has a slide rule in his hand.

Chapter 1Play · one neutron, then k✓ cleared

Everything depends on one number.

A neutron hits a uranium nucleus and the nucleus splits, throwing out two or three new neutrons. Some escape the pile. Some are eaten by cadmium or by impurities in the graphite. The rest split more uranium.

On average, how many new neutrons does each neutron lead to? Fermi called that number . Grow a family of neutrons and watch what does.

Chapter 2Play · build the pile✓ cleared

Why it had to be so big.

This is a toy pile. Yellow dots are uranium lumps sitting in graphite. Tap inside it to fire neutrons in. A neutron that reaches the edge escapes. One that touches a yellow cadmium rod is eaten.

Make the pile bigger and a neutron has to travel further to escape, so it gets more chances to hit uranium first. That's why Fermi's team kept adding layers until the counters told them to stop.

Chapter 3Play · the last rod✓ cleared

Pull it out six inches at a time.

After lunch, every rod is out except one. Fermi has it pulled out step by step. After each step the counters climb and then level off, and he checks the level against his slide rule. A pile below critical always levels off. A pile above critical never does.

Now you're in charge. Time here runs 20 times faster than real life.

STEP 4 · WHY ℓ IS 0.1 SECONDS

Most neutrons from a split fly out almost instantly, within about a ten-thousandth of a second. But about 0.65% come out seconds later, released by the broken fragments as they decay. Those late neutrons stretch the average generation to about s.

Why the room was safeWithout the late neutrons, the same pile would double in about a tenth of a second instead of two minutes. The slow, steady rise everyone watched that afternoon was only possible because of them.
